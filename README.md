# Rules Engine for SillyTavern

Code decides outcomes; the narrator writes them. Each turn:

1. **Question pass.** One model call reads the scene and names the outcomes in doubt (a climb that may fail, a sound that may be missed, a price, time passing). The engine decides each with its rule tools.
2. **Narration.** The narrator gets one system message: the story time, each tracked body, what each character knows, and the decided outcomes ([DECIDED]). Your prompt, card and preset stay as they are.
3. **Rule check.** One model call compares the reply with the decided outcomes and redoes the reply once when it contradicts one.

## Install

1. SillyTavern → Extensions → Install extension.
2. URL `https://github.com/Ndevai/multihog-dev`, branch `rules-standalone`.
3. AI Response Configuration → tick **Enable function calling** (used when the question pass is off or fails).
4. Extensions → **Rules Engine**: the three boxes are on by default.
5. Give the chat a date and place. Type `/rules-time 1850-03-14 18:40` (or `Day 1 08:00`), or let the scenario state them ("London, 14 March 1850, evening"). The weather, prices and daylight need both. The time is read on the place's own clock for that date (summer time, or local mean time before standard time), and daylight follows the Sun and the recorded cloud of that hour. Characters placed on the map (Layout) hear and see what others do nearby, through walls and over a room's noise.

Tested with GLM-5.3 on NVIDIA's API (structured output on). A model without JSON-schema output reads the plan less reliably.

## Settings

In SillyTavern's Extensions menu, in four groups: Engine, Box & page, Sound, Story. Each setting has a short name and one line saying what it does. The engine's:

| Setting | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Rules engine | on | registers the tools and sends the engine's message each turn |
| Question pass | on | one model call before each reply |
| Rule check | on | one model call after each reply |
| Connection | same as the chat | a Connection Manager profile for the two extra calls (e.g. a faster model) |
| Reply length | the connection's own | max tokens for the two extra calls |
| Rules as suggestions | off | for a world far from real history: the outcomes reach the narrator as likely rather than fixed, the story and world book decide, and the rule check redoes nothing |

## Commands

| Command | What it does |
|---|---|
| `/rules-time [time]` | show or set the story time |
| `/rules-log` | this turn's decisions: each call, its arguments, its random draws and result |
| `/rules-replay` | replays every recorded change and says whether it rebuilds the current state |
| `/world-add <line>` | add a line to this card's world book; the model drafts the entry, you approve it |
| `/world-without <person>, <year>` | removes a person from history from that year; their inventions go to others, later, or never |
| `/world-event <event>; <people>; <year>` | removes the named people from that year |
| `/world-earlier <invention>, <year>` | brings an invention earlier, if a first inventor that far ahead is drawn |
| `/world-list` | shows the world book |
| `/rules-seed [code]` | starts the story from a seed: with no code it rolls one. A code like `EW1881-BH.RA-N4-D2-K5-7f2c` gives that start again: place and year, the world's law on axes (the human functions its body lacks, B breath, H warmth, S sleep, E food, D drink, P pain, F fear; a dot; how one came to be, B born so, M made, R came back from death, E from elsewhere; how it passes, A looks like anyone, C copies one person, H keeps out of sight; with E, a dot and its diet, F flesh or B blood, then K (the living, killed), S (the living, spared, blood only), R (the dead, flesh only) or O (their own kind): the engine works out the least it must take from their mass (W, in kg, 70 when left out) and whether they keep warmth of their own, and where feeding kills, how deadly they are follows from it, so D is left out, as in `EW1881-HE.RA.FR-W35-N2-K5-7f2c`; the start says what the world does to the living: still growing, falling and halving in so many years, or gone within a year; or WB1… for your world book's kinds),  how many are of the kind (N4: one person in 10,000, a share of the people there and then), how deadly they are (D2: 10 deaths per 100,000 people a year, the unit of real homicide rates), K and 3–7 people around you, the draw. No name is stored for the kind: people in the story call it after what they noticed, and the names spread from the people who first said them. A card shows what your character knows: you, the people around you with their ties and wants, and the name you use for the kind, if any; the world's law, which your character may not know, is folded under a tap, with its sheet and numbers; the seed's code stands last, to copy. Who is truly what, and the secrets, you meet in the story. Best in a new chat. |

## Good to know

- In a story set in England and Wales between 1841 and 1921, each person the story meets gets a usual day: when they rise and sleep, and their hours of work if they have a trade (1880s hours, Saturday ending at two, Sundays and bank holidays off). While your character is elsewhere, time passing puts them to sleep and to work by it, and the narrator is told where they are; your own character has none.
- Memory keeps meaning, not wording: as a memory ages, a detail that is lost is held as the kind of thing it was (a lodger as a tenant, a servant as a worker, WordNet's kinds), one that changes comes out as a neighbour (Saturday as Friday, 27 as 25, another of its kind that the narrator chooses), and the names people use for the kind drift into one another. A lost detail settles on the everyday word for it, as memory does (a poodle as a dog, a vicar as a priest): the most used word among it and the kinds above it. A word that is already the everyday one (town, blood) is held only as "somewhere" or "something", and the narrator says it in their own words. A name can sit on the tip of the tongue: a character knows who they mean, can sometimes say the first letter or how many syllables, and the name will not come for the hour; more often for a name they have not used in a while, and seven times as often at 70 as at 20 (once a day against once a week). The narrator is told, and your Memory tab shows it when it is your character's. Your story's own words can be linked: the keys of each entry in the card's or the chat's lorebook name one thing, so a misremembered one comes out as another ("the inn" as "the Red Lion"); `/memory-link the Red Lion = Pike's = the inn` adds such a group, `/memory-link the returned : monster` makes a forgotten word come back as its kind, `/memory-link` lists them and `/memory-link remove 2` removes one. The Story window shows each memory as your character holds it now, with how each detail drifted and the words it was first held in under a tap.
- In a seeded story the Story window has a World tab: the people of England and Wales this year and each year since the seed's (births, deaths and those who left as the Registrar General recorded them, landing on each census from 1841 to 1921), how many of them are of the kind, and how many the kind killed; the kind's own details folded under it, as the seed's, which your character may not know.
- The kind's number on the World tab now changes as its sheet says new ones come: the born have children of the kind; the made are made from the living, 1 in 100 of their number a year; of the returned, 1 in 100 of the dead come back; of the incomers, 1 in 100 of their number arrive a year. All of them die and leave as people do, and a kind that kills to eat starves when the living run out.
- The World tab and the narrator also say how the country lives with the kind that year: from how many deaths (or opened graves) the living find against about 1 murder in 100,000 a year, a stage (unnoticed, a run of killings, a panic, an emergency, collapse); how many know its name as a tale and how many blame the deaths on it; what 1881 had to meet it (coroners, the county police, the cheap daily papers, Medical Officers of Health) and what history shows such a stage brings (Whitechapel 1888, New England's exhumations, the Habsburg reports of 1732); and fiction's pattern for it, marked as fiction's.
- The people around you who want something toward one another (the seed's wants) meet while your character is away, when both are awake and free: about every other day (0.46 a day: 7.4 conversations a day with 16 people over a fortnight, Wheeler & Nezlek 1977), three times a day under one roof. Both remember meeting; the one who wants remembers acting on it (a secret when the want is); what was said is the story's to tell when it surfaces.
- A seed of any year from 1841 to 1921 now reads that year: the World tab counts from it (censuses from 1841, births and deaths from 1850), the outcome and the dead a kind can eat use its year's births and deaths, the start card gives that year's people, and the town is drawn by 1891's spread from 1886 on.
- One of a kind whose sheet sees in the dark takes the light as six times brighter, as a cat's eyes do against ours: by starlight they make out a figure at 20 m that a person half the time misses; in daylight it changes nothing, and Look says so. One of a kind that keeps no warmth of its own takes the temperature around it in the body engine: on a frosty night their body sits a degree or so above the air, they spend a small part of the food a warm body burns against the cold, and no hypothermia or heat stroke touches them, while frostbite and burns still do; brought into a warm room they take most of a day to warm through. One of a kind the seed makes active by night keeps the night: their whole day, sleep and trade, runs twelve hours later, as a night shift would, so they sleep while your character is about and feed and meet others by night; and no one feeds in their sleep. A seed also draws the town your character lives in, as people of 1881 lived: 3 in 10 in the twenty towns of over 100,000, more than half in towns of over 10,000, the rest in smaller towns and villages (The Statesman's Year-Book 1899, from the census). The start's card names it ("a town of about 13,000 people"). Where the kind leaves bodies or opens graves, it does so in that town at its own rate (what the living find a year for 100,000 people, times the town's people, less what your circle's own feeders take), at random, wherever your character is. What is found by eight the next morning becomes one record a morning (one body described, more counted), and everyone in your circle hears it in their own time and version. The narrator and the World tab say how much is found there a year and how much since the story began. A half-remembered date is said as people say one: "one Sunday morning in February 1881", "in 1880".
- `/rules-looks` shows the faces every character's picture uses, the same in every chat: the fewest that tell the most, built from facial action units as emotion research predicts them from how a character sees what happened (whether it came suddenly, and for something bad whether anyone could change it) and from the body (pain, sleepiness, closed eyes). `/rules-looks recompute` adds this chat's record of how characters looked to the earlier chats' and works the set out again. With no record yet: calm with a faint smile, a faint frown, a frown, a frown at what nobody can change, a frown at what someone else could change, and a face at something unappealing.
- `/rules-faces` draws those faces for a character from their card picture, only the face redrawn, once each, and the picture above the box then shows the face for how that character looks now. It needs SillyTavern's Image Generation set to Forge or AUTOMATIC1111 with the ADetailer extension (reached straight, or through SillyTavern's CORS proxy: `enableCorsProxy: true` in config.yaml), or to a ComfyUI workflow that takes the character's picture (then run it in that character's own chat). The face is told to the image model in Danbooru's words, which have none for a wrinkled nose, a raised upper lip, raised cheeks, a raised chin or the nostrils' moves, so those parts of a face are left out for now. `/rules-faces Mary` draws another character's. With no image program of your own (SillyTavern on a phone, say), set Image Generation to AI Horde: it is free, but each face waits about ten minutes in its queue, two at a time; the first time, `/rules-faces` asks you to drag a box over the character's face on their picture, and only that part is redrawn. Animagine XL draws them unless you pick another Horde model; a free AI Horde key in the extension's settings puts them ahead of the anonymous queue. You need not run it at all: with AI Horde, Forge or AUTOMATIC1111 set, with Picture: large on (it starts off), each character who appears gets their calm face and the face they are in drawn in the background while you play, and every other face the first time it is needed; until it is ready the box shows their picture. On AI Horde a note asks you once to tap and mark each character's face. Someone the engine made, who has no card, first gets a portrait drawn once from what the engine knows of them (sex, age, build, the era and their trade; Forge or AUTOMATIC1111 only), and their faces are made from it. Their eye colour comes from six genes that set the eye's melanin, each copy drawn as common as it is among the people they grew up among, through a published forensic model; their hair, from what was recorded of people with eyes like theirs (9,080 of England and Wales in the 1870s and 80s, 546 Turks and Armenians for someone from the Middle East); their hair greying with age as a survey of 4,192 people found, their clothes are those of their trade in that year, and the narrator is told the same, so the story and the picture agree. When the story makes someone a character's mother, father, brother or sister, their eyes come from the same genes: children from their parents, brothers and sisters from the same two.
- One of the people around you may truly be of the seed's kind. If it lives on flesh or blood, it feeds as often as its body needs (the seed's own numbers: a warm blood-drinker that kills about every 2.7 days, a cold one every 18), only while your character is elsewhere and outside its hours of work. You learn of it only through the story: the feeding is its secret, and what the town finds (a body, an opened grave) reaches each person around you in their own time, from someone who heard it from someone: about half the same day, most by the next, a few never; and each telling drops or bends some of it, so each holds their own version (a four-year-old girl may reach someone as a woman).

- Every number the engine uses has a published source or is marked [ASSUMED]. Sources and decisions: `docs/progress.md`, `docs/df-body-engine.md`; coverage: `docs/features.md`.
- Each turn costs two extra model calls (question pass and rule check), three when the rule check redoes a reply.
- A refused outcome ("State unchanged") is the engine asking for a missing number; the question pass asks again once.
- Under your message a folded line shows how the engine read it, one tagged call each ("Sight Tom · 15 m", "Body vault the low wall · 46 %"), the tag's colour for the kind of rule; click it to open, hover a row to see the rule call. It stays with the message, in a book serif that follows the story's era (Garamond for the 1500s, the Fell types, Baskerville, a Victorian Modern, Caslon; Georgia from 1970 or with no date). When your words leave a choice open, the likeliest reading is taken and shown ("☞ walk after the thief in the open — or…") unless a wrong guess would hurt someone ("I hit him" with two men at the bar, or "I look at Tom and Will, then I punch him"): then the engine asks in a sentence ("Do you mean to hit ¹Tom or ²Will?"; click or press the number), or "Say it another way" stops the reply. Opened, a guess offers the others as links; one puts "no, catch up with the thief" in your input box. That line, or one of your own such as "no, Will" or "I meant to keep out of sight", redoes the last turn that way.
- How you say a line: select words in the message box and pick from the toolbar that appears (rises, louder, softer, whispered, slower, a pause, breathy, smiling, a breath in, angry…), or press ♪ beside the box for the word at the cursor; Tab from the box enters the toolbar while it shows. The marks stay out of sight: the box shows your words, the marked ones softly underlined, and the chat sets your line the way it sounds. The engine reads every mark exactly: a whispered or shouted line carries as far as that voice does, and the listener hears your feeling right as often as people do, sometimes hearing another. With **Multiple distinctions** on (the default), each part of the voice has a place of its own in the chat: a thin stroke above a word for its pitch (a rise-fall and a fall-rise differ), heavier letters for louder and paler ones for softer, a wavy rule under creaky words, dots under breathy ones and a broken rule under whispered ones, wider gaps for slower and a lean for faster, a drawn-out vowel written again ("Nooo"), narrow letters for falsetto, and the feeling's colour at the line's edge. Every line keeps the story's line spacing. Hover a marked word to read its marks with their sizes ("louder (about 6 dB above your normal voice)"); on a phone, hold it, and in the text box the explanation stands in the menu line until you let go (a tap still goes on). The word at the name tag's right explains itself the same way. Turn it off in the settings for the lighter look.
- The chat is a page of its own: with **Dialogue page** on (the default), messages are set black on ivory in a book serif that follows the story's era, with no letter shadow and room between the lines for the voice marks, whatever your theme; quoted words keep the text colour, as in print. After your name a word says what your character is doing in the line: SPEAKING when the line holds said words (a speech call, or words in quotation marks), THINKING when your character works something out; hover it for the call it rests on. Off, messages keep the theme's look and the word stays.
- In the text box, the type is large enough to read at full speed with the phone at arm's length (0.2° of letter height at 58 cm), and a line above the chat says where and when the scene is and its light ("Recife · Sunday 15 July 1990, 18:20 · nautical twilight") instead of your device's clock; scrolling the box no longer pulls the page behind it.
- The text box has a visual novel's look by default ("The box's look: night"): a see-through dark panel in white corner brackets under a pink name tag, white spoken words and light blue narration, and a blinking ▼ after the last word when the line has finished appearing. Along the box's foot, signs a finger can hit (each 44 px): back and next with the place between them, then auto (fast-forward), music (a note), story (a book) and log (a clock). Words that name what the engine decided are gold letters in it. On a short screen (a phone held sideways) the box fits under the scene line and its lines scroll. The name tag keeps a name on one line: whole where it fits beside SPEAKING, cut with "…" where it does not; holding the tag shows the whole name. "page" keeps the page's own look.
- The box's Story button opens a window over the dimmed screen, the same size each time, its tabs at the foot: what your character remembers, one line each with a sign for how they learned it and ≈ where some of its details have drifted or are lost (a tap opens how and when they learned it, how long ago, how sure they are, how clearly they see it, and the drift; a faint line along the row is as long as they still recall it), a small map of the scene around them (who they heard and saw this turn), and the story's time (a tap on a point goes back to that line; a tap on the clock opens it too). A tap on a name tag opens that person's report card, a screen of its own like the first meeting's: their picture, their trade under their name, then labelled rows (age, height, build, voice, feeling now) and what your character remembers of them; it is drawn anew each time, so it follows the story. The large picture glows in the colour of the feeling of the one it shows.
- Every kind has a stat sheet with every field filled (docs/kinds.md): what its body lacks, its mass and warmth, Dwarf Fortress's attributes, senses, speed, mind, active hours, lifespan, how new ones come, what it resists, what it eats and how it attacks, and what shows. Values come from the Dwarf Fortress creature files inside the engine where they can, and a human's value, written down, where nothing is known; a kind's strength scales its grip.
- A world-book kind that passes for human can have tells, written like laws of nature (a breath that leaves no mist in cold air, clean nails after a long road, no heartbeat under a hand). Who truly is what is fixed first and kept hidden; looking at or touching someone shows what your character can make out at that distance, in that light and air, and the sign shows whatever its bearer wants. Tells known to all are listed in the world; the rest you find out.
- The narrator is told to tell the story plainly, as a witness would: a concrete detail carries what is strange, and a death is told in the same voice as a meal.
- What happened and what a character remembers are kept apart: a detail they were misled about stays their own memory, and receipts, signs, places and other people keep to what happened. Shown what happened, the memory gives way about 6 times in 10, and about 9 in 10 when they are also shown how they came to remember it wrong. A forgotten fact can come back while they stand where they learned it.
- Memories change as they age, the way measured memories do: weeks and months on, a character tells some details of an event changed or leaves them out (the engine says how many, the narrator picks which), and stays as sure of it as before when it mattered to them, less sure when it did not. Where they got a fact, seen or told and by whom, is held less well than the fact itself and goes with it.
- A knob by the message box sets how closely the story follows the line you are about to send, from 0 (your line is an idea; the narrator writes your character's turn its own way) through 0.5 (small touches added), 1.0 (exactly your acts, the default), 1.5 (your acts in your order and your words as you wrote them) to 2.0 (your line stands as written and the reply begins after it). Tap it to turn it one stop, drag it up or down, or use the wheel or the arrow keys. Below 1.0 the engine takes the likeliest reading of your words; above 1.0 it asks whenever two readings fit. From 1.0 the rule check redoes a reply that departs from your line. The line keeps its stop (a mark after your name) and the knob returns to 1.0.
- Select words in a message (a long press on a phone, a drag with a mouse) and their path opens in a small card above the box (never over it), one step a row with a sign: what your character heard, saw or was told, by whom and when; the outcome the engine decided; or the narrator's own words, with the story's clock beside words of light and time. "the measures" under it holds the numbers (distance, loudness, the dice). A tap on the chat closes it.
- The first time a character with a character card of their own speaks, the screen is theirs alone: their picture large, its shadow sliding out behind it, their name on a tilted white label and their trade (or age) above it on a pink band, as visual novels introduce people; a tap goes on. It plays once a chat, on their first line in the whole chat, also when only the chat's last messages are on the page; the story's time and place sit in a badge in the corner, and the box's buttons are words along its bottom edge.
- Characters speak their lines aloud as they type (setting "Voices: natural", the default): each voice is computed from the speaker's body (the pitch their vocal folds give, their throat's length, their pace), applied to the averaged way of speaking of 16 people talking freely, so a tall man sounds low, a small child high and slow, and any body gives its own voice. It needs `voices/averaged-person.bin` (handed over separately; see voices/README.md) and downloads the speech model once (165 MB); the first line of a session waits for it (about 8 s on a desktop), a tap shows a waiting line at once. Narration and whispers stay silent. A character's feeling (from the Appraise tool, or a voice mark in the line) colours their voice by its strength: anger high and strained, sadness and tenderness narrow and slow, with `voices/emotion-<feeling>.bin` (handed over separately) in place. "robotic" speaks with eSpeak NG; "blips" gives one tone a syllable; "off" silences them. Each character also has their own way of speaking, drawn once against people of their sex from 96 recorded speakers: how widely their melody moves, how much of their speech is pauses, how breathy they sound.
- One thing on screen at a time: with **Text box** on (the default), the chat shows one box at the foot of the screen under the name of whoever says it. A new box starts wherever said words begin or end, and a long stretch goes on in the next box. Said words are black (white on the dark page) with SPEAKING, or WHISPERING, at the plate's right; everything else is blue, with no name on the tag (a name always means someone's words), and THINKING with your character's name when you work something out. Letters appear one by one, said words at the speaker's pace and the rest at reading pace; a tap shows the rest at once, then the next box. Auto moves on by itself once a box has been read, back or ← goes back, and log shows the whole chat. In the box, SillyTavern's top bar folds away under the ▲ at the top right. The narrator names who says each quotation, so the plate can show it; in your own lines, the person acting just before a quotation says it.
- Words that name what the engine decided are marked in gold, as important words are in yellow in a visual novel. **Page colours** offers the light page or a dark one (white, light blue and gold on black), and **The speaker's picture** can show the character card's picture large above the box.
- **Music**: add your own songs in the settings. Each is measured as you add it (tempo, loudness, brightness, key), and the song nearest the feelings in the scene plays: your line marked sad brings a calm song in a minor key, a character's joy a lively one in a major key. Give a song a set named after a character or a place, and it becomes their theme. When several feelings are in the scene, the strongest counts 90% and the newest 10%. A song stays while the scene stays in its area, unless another song is clearly nearer, and the next one fades in over 4 seconds. The ♪ button turns the music on and off.
- A character's age is evidence about their growth: each person gets their own growth timing, drawn once, and under 19 their height and build are the share of their adult size they have reached at that age, so two 14-year-olds can be a child and a near-adult.
- Each character's voice is computed from their body once the engine knows their height, sex and age (from 5; a boy's voice drops across his own puberty): a simulation of their airways (Praat's articulatory synthesizer, ported) moves air through their vocal folds and tract, and the pitch is how fast the folds open, the formants how the tract resonates; each person also gets their own tract size and habitual fold stretch, drawn once. The first run for a character takes a few seconds in the background, and the narrator waits for it. Their spoken words appear in the box at their syllables a second, the narrator reads the voice in the body notes, and hovering a name shows it.
- "Say it another way" puts your line back in the box, marks and all, and takes it out of the chat; the next line stands in its place.
- People placed on the map overhear what is said near them, as far as the voice carries to each, and remember what they make out.
- Water below 0 °C is not ice by its temperature alone: the Substance lookup decides whether it freezes from how much there is, what is in it and how long it has been cold, so a cloud droplet at −1 °C stays liquid, a bucket freezes, and water touching frost freezes at once.
- For people placed on the map, a blow, a choke hold, a pistol shot, a chase, a look, speech and a sound take their distance and walls from the map, not from the reply's words: a punch thrown across the room falls short and says which walk closes the gap, and a wall with every door shut stops a blow or a shot.
- Code writes calls for your character's own acts: in "Tom punches Mara" or "I tell Will to wait an hour" the other person's act is read from the scene; "she" in a line written in the third person is your character when the line names no one else she could be. Acts the line says are not done ("I don't hit him", "I almost fall") and out-of-character asides ("(OOC: …)") are not acted on. Typed ›guillemets‹, «guillemets» and „German quotes“ are quotation marks, read as speech like "…", and capitals read as louder on common words only (STOP, NOOOO; NATO and ICU stay as written, and so do the WHO and the IT desk). You can speak to a group as to a person ("I shout to the crew", "I tell the town council"), and to someone named with their looks ("the old blind beggar").
- A line in the third person ("Mara climbs the wall") or in asterisks is read by its verbs like one in the first person, and words in bold or italics ("I punch **Tom**", "I _whisper_ to Will") are read as the words; a line that opens "No, I …" or "No, Will is …" is an ordinary line, and only a short "no, Will" or "I meant …" redoes the last turn. Regenerating after a reply that failed or was stopped, or pressing Send with the box empty, runs the turn once; a line edited first runs as edited. Deleting a reply keeps what your line did: the line stays in the chat. Each line keeps the world it started from, so a swipe, a regenerate or a "no, Will" starts from the line itself, also after messages before or after it are deleted. Curly apostrophes read as straight ones ("I’ll punch him"). A number in brackets ("Tom (35)") and a temperature ("20 °C") stay words; "I drop the sword" and "I fall asleep" are no falls. "I sit by the fire for an hour" passes the hour, "I swing my axe at the orc" is a blow, and "I give Will 3 coins" hands three. "I fire my pistol at the guard", "I open fire" and "I gun him down" are shots, a handgun's among them; "I fire the cook", "I fire the kiln" and "I hold my fire" are none, and "a shot of whiskey" is a measure. The models are shown only the options a scene can hold: an 1850 scene is offered no sneakers, antibiotics or spells, and the narrator gets the tools your line needs. Results reach the narrator as what happened and why, without sound levels, status tags or chances. A verb is read by what its object is: "I charge the cook two shillings" is a price, "I hold a meeting" is no grip, "I cut the cook" cannot break him like a plank, and "I charge the cook" is left to the scene, which weighs it: a sword in your hand, a fight going on, the words around it and how "charge" was meant earlier in the chat each make one reading likelier. "I run" runs at the pace runners choose, "I dash" sprints and "I hurry" walks as fast as you can walk; "5 m to the door, then 10 m to the gate" is walked in both legs.
- The tools a turn is offered follow the scene as well as your verbs: smoke in the hallway, a freezing lake, a desert at noon, a camp at 4,500 m, a sentry on gravel, a guard who shouted, a pan on the fire each add the tool that decides them.
